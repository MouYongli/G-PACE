# GRACE: A Graph Memory–Enhanced Context Engine with Query-Defined Views over Bitemporal RDF for LLM Agents

### Context is a scarce resource

The conversation history of an LLM agent grows without bound, while the context window available at each step is limited. Larger windows help only in part. Compute and cost per call grow with input length. Models use long inputs unevenly, and accuracy drops when the relevant information sits in the middle of the input [1]; the effective context length is often far shorter than the nominal one [2]. Giving the LLM the whole history at every step is therefore expensive, and it is not guaranteed to be the most accurate option either.

The history is cumulative and append-only, but the state it describes keeps changing: later messages may revise, invalidate or supersede earlier information. This paper studies one problem that follows from this. When is the context derived from the history and handed to the LLM still valid, when must it be rebuilt, and is maintaining a graph for this purpose worth its cost?

### Existing approaches and their limitations

Existing systems reduce the input to the LLM in three ways, and each fails in its own way when the conversation state keeps changing.

- **Compression.** The history, or its older part, is condensed into a shorter text: rolling or recursive summaries [3], hierarchical paging of memory in and out of the context [4], the automatic compaction built into agent frameworks, and token-level prompt compression [5]. A summary does not record which part of the history each of its sentences depends on. When a new message revises a fact the summary relies on, the summary becomes wrong and the system cannot tell; the only safe remedy is a full rewrite, which brings back the cost that compression was meant to avoid. Compression is also lossy: details it drops cannot be recovered from the summary.
- **Retrieval.** The history is kept in an external store, and at each step a few passages are retrieved, typically by fusing the rankings of lexical matching with BM25 [6] and dense retrieval [7], for example with Reciprocal Rank Fusion [8]. A system offer and a user request are close both lexically and semantically, and ranking cannot tell which of them sets the current constraint. Questions about sets, counts, the latest state or the state at a past time require combining scattered evidence, and a ranked list of passages does not guarantee that all of it is retrieved.
- **Structured memory.** Facts are extracted into memory entries, state tables or graphs and queried when a question arrives [9, 10]. Most systems invalidate an old fact only when a new fact conflicts with it directly, and do not track whether content derived from those facts is still correct. A new statement often overwrites an old one regardless of who made it and whether it is the kind of statement that should change the state.

These failure modes point to three limitations: derived context goes stale without any signal; attribution is lost; and scattered evidence is not brought together.

### Closest work and the gap

Each ingredient of a remedy has precedents. Zep/Graphiti keeps two timelines on every edge, traces edges back to episodes and lets an LLM invalidate edges that conflict with new ones; its community summaries are updated when nodes are added and corrected by periodic full refresh [10]. APEX-MEM stores facts append-only and resolves conflicts at query time, with no stored derived content [11]. A line of 2026 preprints cascades invalidation along dependency edges between memory records [12, 13, 14, 15]. In all of them the dependency points to records or versions that the derived object has already consumed, so a new record that the object never consumed, such as a new member of a set, triggers nothing.

Two recent diagnoses already treat derived memory as views over evolving data. MemShift shows that dependency keys built from citations miss records that enter an item's scope after the write, traces the misses to the phantom problem of predicate locks, and finds that a coarse source key misses nothing but invalidates almost every unchanged item [16]. MemDerive expresses derived state as views over tables and finds that memory systems refresh far fewer implicit changes than explicit ones [17]. Outside agent memory, Martin, Unbehauen and Auer invalidate cached SPARQL results, and the application objects rendered from them, whenever a changed triple matches one of the query's triple patterns, and prove this rule sound by structural induction [18].

The gap that remains is therefore narrow, and we state it as such. We adopt the pattern-matching invalidation rule and its compositional soundness argument and claim neither as new; lifting the matching unit from a triple pattern to a claim pattern is an implementation detail. Our novelty rests on three points: a definition of staleness at two levels, answers and citations; a formalisation of this definition and of its sound decision on an append-only, bitemporal RDF graph whose writes include attributions and evidence links; and an evaluation on natural dialogue with noisy extracted graphs and a complete token ledger. Two-level staleness has only partial precedent, in the maintenance of provenance relations, where no work we read treats a change of witnesses or evidence as a level of staleness of a derived artefact. The evidence also cuts against us in two places. Giving the LLM the full history remains a strong baseline [9], and MemShift's query-time arm, which stores nothing, is cheap [16]. Our evaluation treats both as first-class competitors.

### Our approach

We present GRACE (GRaph memory–enhAnced Context Engine), a context engine for LLM agents. A full agent has further modules, such as a control loop and tools; we set them aside and study only how the context is built from the conversation history.

GRACE has two layers. The **memory layer** only stores: the raw message log with its lexical and dense text indexes, the claim graph and the views. The **pipeline layer** runs the write path, which updates the memory after each message, and the read path, which assembles the working context for the current task. The LLM is outside GRACE: GRACE sends it the working context through an API, and the LLM generates the answer from this context alone, so the same answering LLM can be used unchanged with every baseline. The method has two parts.

- **A graph channel on top of hybrid retrieval.** The claim graph is append-only and bitemporal, and is represented in RDF with standard vocabularies: RDF 1.2 reifiers [19], so that extracted claims are not asserted by default; PROV-O [20] for provenance; and SHACL [21] for structural validation. Each claim carries a valid time and a knowledge version and is linked to its evidence spans and to attributions that record the speaker and the communicative function of each statement (request, offer, accept, reject, confirm, inform and others, following ISO 24617-2 [22]). An *attribution guard* lets a new claim supersede an earlier one only when the speaker and the communicative function agree and the statement is the speaker's own; otherwise both claims are kept. A supersession event has the kind *change* (the world changed) or *correction* (the earlier claim was mistaken), so both the current state and the state at a past time can be queried. For each task, a typed intermediate representation is compiled deterministically into a restricted fragment of SPARQL 1.1 [23], with evidence sets projected by construction; the results are fused with lexical and dense candidates and assembled into a working context within a token budget.
- **Query-defined views.** Reusable derived context is stored as views. Each view is defined by a query; its content is the query result, turned into text by template serialisation or by LLM rendering, with evidence cited for every sentence, and the view records its cut-off version. After each new message is committed, the system matches the change set against the claim patterns of each defining query, including the patterns inside negated conditions and aggregations, and marks the views that may be stale. At the next read, sentences whose answers changed are regenerated, and a program patches the citations of sentences whose evidence alone changed, without an LLM call. When staleness detection fails, the engine falls back to raw text.

The second part rests on an observation. A policy that invalidates a view only when a claim it used is superseded misses three kinds of change: a new claim that matches the query; a write that supersedes nothing but matches a negated condition, such as an acceptance that removes a candidate from "offered and not yet accepted"; and a change in the members of an aggregate. In an append-only graph all three are insertions outside the entries the view has consumed, so on such a store they are the normal case, not an edge case.

GRACE pursues two goals at once. The first is to match or improve on the answer accuracy of the full-history, compression and hybrid-retrieval baselines. The second is to reduce end-to-end token cost, counting every LLM call, failed attempts included, on the write side (extraction, vocabulary maintenance, view rendering) and on the read side (planning, verification, answering).

### Research questions

**RQ1 (graph channel).** On which question types does adding a structured graph channel to hybrid lexical and dense retrieval improve accuracy, and do the gains come from the attribution guard, from structure or from open predicates? We hypothesise gains on attribution-sensitive state tracking, aggregation, multi-hop joins and point-in-time queries, and ties on single-passage lookup. The attribution guard should let the system answer both "what was originally asked" and "what is chosen now", and stop restatements from counting as independent evidence. On open-domain dialogue, a claim graph with open predicates should answer questions a fixed slot table cannot express; on MultiWOZ, whose schema is fixed, the two should tie. We do not claim that a graph beats a relational table with the same fields.
*Overturned if* hybrid retrieval with the same answering LLM and budget ties on these slices. *Narrowed if* hybrid retrieval over chunks tagged with speaker and dialogue act obtains most of the gain attributed to the guard (the conclusion then becomes "speaker and dialogue-act information helps", not "the attribution guard helps"); if a deterministic rule that never writes system values ties with the attribution guard, which shows only that the rule agrees with the annotation convention; or if a fixed slot table ties on open-domain slices, so that open predicates bring no gain.

**RQ2 (view freshness).** When must a view be rebuilt, how fine must the dependencies be, at what cost, and how common is each kind of staleness in natural dialogue? At version $r$, a view with cut-off version $r_0$ is *answer-stale* if the answer set of its defining query differs between $r_0$ and $r$, and *citation-stale* if the answers are unchanged but their witnesses (why-provenance) or evidence differ. We hypothesise that citation-based dependencies miss new matches, writes that supersede nothing but match a negated condition, and changes in aggregate members; that claim-pattern-level decisions agree with full recomputation and over-mark less than predicate-level decisions; and that citation staleness is far more frequent than answer staleness in natural dialogue. We compare eight decision strategies on the same claim graph: never invalidating; full rebuild after every message; used claims only; used claims plus the patterns inside negation and aggregation; predicate level; claim-pattern matching at write time (push); LLM-free re-query with fingerprints of answers, witnesses and evidence at read time (pull), which is exact with respect to the claim graph and serves as the reference; and push filtering with read-time confirmation.
*Narrowed if* the three kinds of change that citation-based dependencies miss are rare in natural dialogue, or if read-time re-query with fingerprints is not more expensive in any stratum, which is likely on dialogue-scale graphs. The contribution then narrows to the staleness criterion, the measured natural distribution and their evaluation, stated as "exact staleness checks are cheap; push-based matching pays off only beyond the crossover found by the scale experiment".

**RQ3 (accuracy and cost).** When does the accuracy–cost trade-off favour GRACE, and is caching LLM-rendered views necessary? The cost claim rests on a premise that we test first: the answering LLM answers more accurately from LLM-rendered views than from template-serialised fact cards. Given that premise, we hypothesise that when the same history is read many times, revisions occur and the context budget is tight, write cost is amortised and cache-adjusted cumulative cost is below that of the full history, a summary rewritten at every update, and "no cache, resolve at query time". The competitors are the full history (with and without prefix caching); compression (a rolling summary rewritten after every update, and an old summary reused without rewriting); hybrid retrieval, a fixed slot table, and an append-only memory without views that resolves conflicts at query time; and GRACE's own template-serialisation variant, which re-runs the query at every read with no rendering and no caching. We vary reads per history, revision density and context budget, report the payback read count $M^{*}$ in every regime, and mark where GRACE does not win.
*Overturned if* template serialisation is not worse in answer accuracy, so that rendering, caching and maintenance are all unnecessary and the paper is about "a staleness criterion plus computing at query time"; if the savings come only from a shorter answer prompt and vanish once writing, compilation, validation and failed retries are counted; or if $M^{*}$ exceeds the actual read counts in the benchmarks.

Retrieval channels, fusion, routing, evidence-driven assembly, vocabulary evolution, writer scale and the invocation of LLM call types are studied as ablations, each with its own narrowing condition, and are not headline claims.

### Contributions

1. **Claim and evidence model.** An append-only, bitemporal RDF claim graph based on standard vocabularies, in which claims are not asserted by default and only the currently accepted state is asserted. Attributions record the communicative function and the report mode as two separate axes, and both guard the supersession rules. The guard is the one element of the model that we have to validate ourselves (RQ1); the other elements are a reasoned combination of existing practice.
2. **Query-defined views and their staleness criterion.** Staleness at two levels, answers and citations, where citation staleness is repaired without an LLM; the three kinds of change that citation-based dependencies miss, with the observation that in an append-only graph all three are insertions outside the entries a view has consumed; a soundness proposition that extends the triple-pattern rule of Martin et al. [18] to negation, counting, latest-state selection and witnesses, with exactness conditions covering four kinds of writes (claims, attributions, evidence links and supersession events); a comparison of eight decision strategies on dialogue workloads, including two arms that, to our knowledge, have not been evaluated in the agent-memory invalidation literature (LLM-free read-time re-query with fingerprints, and push filtering with read-time confirmation); and a measurement of how often each kind of staleness occurs in natural dialogue.
3. **A verifiable path from task to working context.** A deterministic compiler from a typed intermediate representation to a SPARQL fragment that projects evidence sets by construction and annotates empty results and counts with their evidence scope; fusion of graph results with lexical and dense candidates; and assembly of source sentences and fact cards within a budget, driven by evidence requirements.
4. **Quality-matched end-to-end cost and layered diagnosis.** A comparison of three sources of view content (template serialisation, LLM rendering, LLM rewriting), which decides whether cached views are needed at all; cache-adjusted payback curves and a maintenance-cost surface over revision density and read count; cost per correct answer including failed attempts; a staleness-detection evaluation stratified by change type, with decision misses and extraction-induced misses counted separately; and a comparison between gold and extracted graphs, together with a direct evaluation of the extracted graph.

Provenance links, bitemporality, separating requests from offers, the pattern-matching invalidation rule with its soundness argument, and local repair are adopted practice and are not claimed as novel.

### Scope of conclusions

We prepare for negative results in advance. If template-serialised fact cards match LLM-rendered views in answer accuracy, we claim a staleness criterion plus computing at query time, not that maintained views are cheaper. If read-time re-query is not more expensive anywhere in the range of the scale experiment, the conclusion narrows to "exact staleness checks are cheap", and push-based matching is reported as one implementation. If the input to the LLM shrinks but end-to-end tokens grow, the conclusion narrows to better use of the LLM's context window. Results on gold graphs are a correctness check; empirical conclusions come from extracted graphs and the measured natural distribution. Maintenance-cost claims rest only on interleaved reading, and the benchmarks are English everyday and task-oriented dialogues.
